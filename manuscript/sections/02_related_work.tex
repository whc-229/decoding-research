\subsection{Decoders and Representation Bias}
\label{sec:related:repr}

Rothlauf's theory of representations \cite{rothlauf2006representations} explains how a decoder affects a genetic algorithm (GA).
An encoding is \emph{redundant} when several genotypes give the same solution.
If good solutions are represented more often than others, the GA performs better; if less often, it performs worse.
A decoder's bias therefore helps only when it points in the right direction.

Decoders remain the main way to bring problem knowledge into EAs.
Random-key GAs keep the operators problem-independent and put the knowledge into the decoder \cite{londe2025random}.
Weight codings perturb the problem data and let a greedy heuristic decode them \cite{raidl2000weighted}.
Recent decoders for semiconductor fabs \cite{park2026genetic}, steelmaking \cite{jiang2025soft} and integrated process planning and scheduling \cite{jiang2025more} contain more and more problem knowledge,
and an LLM has been used to set instance-specific bias weights inside the decoder of a biased random-key GA \cite{chacon2025llm}.
In all of these, the strength of the bias is a side effect of the decoder's design.
\RDD makes it an explicit parameter, the deviation rate, and Section~\ref{sec:theory} analyzes it.

\subsection{Searching Near a Heuristic}
\label{sec:related:bias}

Storer \etal \cite{storer1992new} proposed two search spaces built around a heuristic.
In the problem space, genes perturb the problem data and the heuristic builds the schedule; in the heuristic space, genes choose which heuristic makes each decision.
GAs over heuristic choices have been applied to job shops \cite{dorndorf1995evolution}, real-world scheduling \cite{hart1998solving} and nurse rostering \cite{aickelin2004indirect}.
In these encodings every decision is open to the search, and no heuristic acts as a default.

Other methods search near a single heuristic.
LDS explores solutions that deviate from a heuristic in at most $k$ decisions \cite{harvey1995lds};
heuristic-biased stochastic sampling favors the heuristic's top choices with a fixed bias \cite{bresina1996hbss};
squeaky wheel optimization repeatedly changes the priorities that drive a greedy constructor \cite{joslin1999squeaky};
and large neighborhood search and iterated greedy destroy part of a solution and rebuild it with a heuristic \cite{shaw1998using,ruiz2007simple}.
Within EAs, the most common way to use a heuristic is to seed the initial population with its solutions \cite{shen2015dfjsp,zhang2011effective}.
Learned policies have also been used to sample \cite{gu2025synergizing} or to guide \cite{chen2026teacher} evolutionary search.

\RDD takes the idea of LDS, a heuristic plus a few deviations, and turns it into an encoding for MOEAs.
Unlike LDS and biased sampling, the deviations are stored in the genotype and evolved; unlike seeding, the rule acts on every genotype; and unlike heuristic-space search, there is a default rule and a parameter that sets how often the search leaves it.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} compares \RDD with these alternatives directly.

\subsection{Learning Construction Rules}
\label{sec:related:rules}

GP hyper-heuristics evolve dispatching rules and remain an active topic.
Recent work keeps the GP population diverse \cite{zhang2026genetic}, trades rule quality against size \cite{pang2025multiform}, learns rules online \cite{nguyen2026towards}, and shares knowledge between populations for different objectives \cite{chen2025optimizing}.
LLMs now write heuristics as code inside an evolutionary loop, following FunSearch \cite{romera2024funsearch}, EoH \cite{liu2024evolution} and ReEvo \cite{ye2024reevo}.
Later methods maintain code diversity \cite{dat2025hsevo}, organize candidates in a search tree \cite{zheng2025monte}, optimize several objectives \cite{yao2025multi}, evolve sets of complementary heuristics \cite{liu2026eohs} or whole metaheuristics \cite{vanstein2025llamea}; HeuriGym benchmarks them \cite{chen2026heurigym}, and two surveys cover the field \cite{liu2026systematic,zhang2026systematic}.
In scheduling, LLMs rewrite the operators of an EA during the run \cite{liao2025online}, build rule portfolios for dynamic shops \cite{zhou2026dsevolve}, generate dispatching rules \cite{may2026llm,huang2025leveraging}, and supply heuristics to NSGA-II \cite{fornies2025remoh}.
Deep reinforcement learning trains policies that build schedules directly \cite{liu2025flexible}.

These works judge a learned rule by the schedules it produces.
In \RDD the rule is the starting point of a search, so we judge it by the schedules that random deviations from it produce (Section~\ref{sec:method:learn}), and we compare GP, neural networks and LLMs as learners for this score.

\subsection{Rescheduling and Multi-Objective Rostering}
\label{sec:related:resched}

When a schedule is disrupted, the rest of it must be revised.
Shen and Yao \cite{shen2015dfjsp} solve dynamic flexible job shops with proactive--reactive MOEAs that include a stability objective and seed the population with the previous schedule.
Recent work uses deep reinforcement learning for predictive--reactive rescheduling \cite{duan2025deep} and combines a competitive EA with double Q-learning for robust scheduling \cite{zhao2024dql}.
In personnel rostering, rerostering after absences has been solved by GAs over permutations \cite{moz2007genetic} and other evolutionary methods \cite{maenhout2011evolutionary},
and a recent review notes that most rostering studies combine their criteria into a weighted sum \cite{bodvarsdottir2026review}.
\RDD puts the old schedule into the decoder instead: it becomes the rule, and every genotype departs from it in only a few shifts.

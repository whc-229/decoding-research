\subsection{Representations, Redundancy and Decoders}
\label{sec:related:repr}

A decoder maps a genotype to a solution, and in scheduling it usually does so by building the schedule step by step.
Rothlauf's theory of representations \cite{rothlauf2006representations} explains how such a map affects search.
A representation is redundant when several genotypes encode the same solution.
Uniform redundancy does not change the expected performance of a GA.
Non-uniform redundancy helps if it over-represents good solutions and hurts if it over-represents poor ones.
Such a bias is therefore useful only with prior knowledge, and it is not robust when the knowledge is wrong.

Indirect encodings with problem-specific decoders remain the main way to apply EAs to scheduling.
Random-key genetic algorithms and their biased variant BRKGA keep the variation operators problem-independent and put all problem knowledge into the decoder \cite{londe2025random}.
Recent decoders encode more knowledge in this way.
Park and Huh \cite{park2026genetic} use a feasibility-agnostic chromosome and a three-phase decoder that turns any chromosome into a schedule that respects queue-time limits in semiconductor fabs.
Jiang \cite{jiang2025soft} proposes a soft encoding for steelmaking scheduling.
Jiang \emph{et al.} \cite{jiang2025more} split the solution into route selection and operation priorities, decoded with a ready-operation set.

These works design a decoder for a problem and judge it by the final result.
They do not measure how the decoder biases the search, and the strength of that bias is not a parameter of the design.
Section~\ref{sec:repr} measures this bias for a sequence of decoders,
and Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes its strength a parameter and analyzes it.

\subsection{Heuristic Bias in Search}
\label{sec:related:bias}

Several classic methods search near a construction heuristic.
Storer \emph{et al.} \cite{storer1992new} search in problem space, where the genes perturb the problem data before a heuristic builds the schedule,
and in heuristic space, where the genes select the heuristic used at each decision.
Aickelin and Dowsland \cite{aickelin2004indirect} evolve the order in which a greedy decoder assigns nurses, and the heuristic knowledge in the decoder is central to the performance of their indirect GA.
In these encodings every decision is touched by a gene.
Two non-evolutionary methods are closer to our idea.
Limited discrepancy search \cite{harvey1995lds} explores, in a search tree, the solutions that depart from a heuristic at no more than $k$ decisions.
Heuristic-biased stochastic sampling \cite{bresina1996hbss} samples solutions with a probability that favors the heuristic's top choices.

Recent work biases evolutionary search with learned policies.
Gu \emph{et al.} \cite{gu2025synergizing} sample solutions from a reinforcement-learning policy, refine them with GA operators, and feed the refined solutions back into training.
Chen \emph{et al.} \cite{chen2026teacher} use trained neural policies as teachers whose action preferences guide the LLM-driven evolution of heuristic programs.
BRKGA biases recombination toward elite parents \cite{londe2025random}.

\SDD shares the idea of limited discrepancy search: a solution is a heuristic plus a few departures from it.
It turns this idea into an encoding for a multi-objective EA.
The departures are genes, their number is an explicit parameter, and Section~\ref{sec:theory} shows how its best value depends on the rule distance and the budget.
Unlike problem- and heuristic-space encodings, the genes touch only a few decisions, and the rule decides the rest.

\subsection{Learning Construction Rules}
\label{sec:related:rules}

\textbf{Genetic programming.}
GP hyper-heuristics evolve dispatching rules and are an active topic in this journal and beyond.
Recent work keeps the GP population diverse with a tabu list of rule behaviors \cite{zhang2026genetic},
trades rule quality against rule complexity in a many-objective setting \cite{pang2025multiform},
learns rules online in the running system \cite{nguyen2026towards},
and transfers knowledge between related scheduling tasks by multitask GP \cite{chen2025optimizing}.

\textbf{Large language models.}
LLMs now design heuristics as code inside an evolutionary loop.
FunSearch \cite{romera2024funsearch}, EoH \cite{liu2024evolution} and ReEvo \cite{ye2024reevo} established the approach.
Later methods add diversity measures \cite{dat2025hsevo}, organize the candidates in a search tree \cite{zheng2025monte},
optimize several objectives such as quality and runtime \cite{yao2025multi},
evolve a set of complementary heuristics instead of one \cite{liu2026eohs},
and generate complete metaheuristics \cite{vanstein2025llamea}.
HeuriGym benchmarks LLM-written heuristics across combinatorial problems \cite{chen2026heurigym},
and two surveys cover the field \cite{liu2026systematic,zhang2026systematic}.
In scheduling, LLMs rewrite the operators of an EA for flexible job shops during the run \cite{liao2025online},
evolve portfolios of dispatching rules with a learned selector for dynamic shops \cite{zhou2026dsevolve},
generate dispatching rules in an actor--critic arrangement \cite{may2026llm},
produce dispatching rules for human--robot collaborative manufacturing \cite{huang2025leveraging},
and supply heuristics to NSGA-II for multi-objective flexible job shops \cite{fornies2025remoh}.

\textbf{Neural construction.}
Deep reinforcement learning trains policies that build schedules directly, for example with graph neural networks for flexible job shops \cite{liu2025flexible}.

In all of these works, the learned rule is used on its own and judged by the objective value of the schedule it builds.
In \SDD, the rule is the bias of a decoder and works together with the deviation slots.
We therefore judge it by the distribution of rosters that random deviations produce around it (Section~\ref{sec:method:proxy}).
Section~\ref{sec:exp-static} compares GP, neural networks and LLMs as learners for this target.
The work closest in spirit is \cite{liao2025online}, which also uses an LLM to change how a GA explores;
it rewrites the variation operators, whereas we change the decoder's bias.

\subsection{Rescheduling and Multi-Objective Rostering}
\label{sec:related:resched}

When a schedule is disrupted, predictive--reactive methods revise it for the rest of the horizon.
Shen and Yao \cite{shen2015dfjsp} solve dynamic flexible job shops with MOEAs that include a stability objective,
and they seed the initial population with the previous schedule, a repaired schedule and their mutants.
Recent work applies deep reinforcement learning to event-driven predictive--reactive multi-objective rescheduling \cite{duan2025deep}
and combines a competitive EA with double Q-learning for robust scheduling under disruptions \cite{zhao2024dql}.
In personnel rostering, rerostering after absences has been solved with GAs on permutations decoded into rosters \cite{moz2007genetic}
and with evolutionary metaheuristics \cite{maenhout2011evolutionary}.
A recent review finds that most multi-objective rostering studies combine the criteria into a weighted sum,
and that methods which keep the trade-off are rarer \cite{bodvarsdottir2026review}.

The methods above use the old schedule as a reference in the objective or as a starting point of the search.
In Section~\ref{sec:exp-rerostering}, we put the old schedule into the decoder instead: it becomes the rule, and every genotype departs from it at only a few shifts.
We keep both rostering objectives and report the hypervolume.

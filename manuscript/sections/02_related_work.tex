This section reviews four lines of work:
how decoders shape evolutionary search, how heuristics have been used to guide search,
how construction rules are learned, and how schedules are repaired after a disturbance.
For each line we state how our method, sparse-deviation decoding (\SDD), differs.

\subsection{Representations, Redundancy and Decoders}
\label{sec:related:repr}

Evolutionary algorithms (EAs) for scheduling often use an indirect encoding:
the genotype is a vector of numbers, and a \emph{decoder} turns it into a schedule, usually by building the schedule step by step.
Rothlauf's theory of representations \cite{rothlauf2006representations} explains how this mapping affects a genetic algorithm (GA).
An encoding is \emph{redundant} when several genotypes give the same solution.
For synonymous redundancy, where the genotypes of one solution are similar to each other, the theory shows the following.
If every solution is represented equally often, redundancy does not change GA performance.
If good solutions are represented more often than others, the GA performs better; if they are represented less often, it performs worse.
Such a bias therefore helps only when it points in the right direction, and it can hurt when it does not.

Decoders remain a common way to bring problem knowledge into EAs.
Random-key GAs keep the variation operators independent of the problem and put all problem knowledge into the decoder;
the biased random-key GA (BRKGA) also favors elite parents in crossover \cite{londe2025random}.
Recent decoders contain more and more knowledge.
Park and Huh \cite{park2026genetic} use a chromosome that ignores feasibility and a three-phase decoder (job assignment, feasibility adjustment, machine allocation) for semiconductor fabs with queue-time limits.
Jiang \cite{jiang2025soft} proposes a soft encoding for steelmaking scheduling.
Jiang \emph{et al.} \cite{jiang2025more} split a solution into route choices and operation priorities and decode it with a set of ready operations.

These works design a decoder for one problem and judge it by the final result.
They do not report how the decoder biases the search, and how strongly it does so is not a design parameter.
In this paper, Section~\ref{sec:motivation} measures this bias for a series of decoders,
and \SDD turns its strength into a parameter that Section~\ref{sec:theory} analyzes.

\subsection{Searching Near a Heuristic}
\label{sec:related:bias}

Several classic methods search near the solution of a construction heuristic.
Storer \emph{et al.} \cite{storer1992new} proposed new search spaces for sequencing problems.
In their problem space, the genes perturb the problem data, and a heuristic then builds the schedule from the perturbed data.
Aickelin and Dowsland \cite{aickelin2004indirect} evolve the order in which a heuristic decoder assigns nurses,
and compare decoders that contain different amounts of heuristic knowledge.
In these encodings, genes affect every decision.
Two methods outside evolutionary computation are closer to our idea.
Limited discrepancy search \cite{harvey1995lds} explores a search tree for solutions that deviate from a heuristic in at most $k$ decisions.
Heuristic-biased stochastic sampling \cite{bresina1996hbss} samples solutions with probabilities that favor the heuristic's top choices.

Recent work guides evolutionary search with learned policies.
Gu \emph{et al.} \cite{gu2025synergizing} sample solutions from a reinforcement-learning policy, improve them with GA operators, and use the improved solutions to train the policy further.
Chen \emph{et al.} \cite{chen2026teacher} use trained neural policies as teachers: their preferred actions guide an evolution of heuristic programs written by a large language model (LLM).

\SDD takes the idea of limited discrepancy search, ``a heuristic plus a few deviations'', and turns it into an encoding for a multi-objective EA (MOEA).
The deviations are stored in the genotype, and their number is an explicit parameter.
Unlike the encodings above, the genes affect only a few decisions, and the rule decides all others.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} shows how the best number of deviations depends on the distance between the rule and good solutions and on the evaluation budget.

\subsection{Learning Construction Rules}
\label{sec:related:rules}

\textbf{Genetic programming.}
Genetic programming (GP) hyper-heuristics evolve dispatching rules, and they remain an active topic in this journal and elsewhere.
Recent work keeps the GP population diverse with a tabu list of rule behaviors \cite{zhang2026genetic},
trades rule quality against rule size in a many-objective setting \cite{pang2025multiform},
learns rules online while the system runs \cite{nguyen2026towards},
and evolves rules for different objectives in separate populations that share knowledge at an adaptive rate \cite{chen2025optimizing}.

\textbf{Large language models.}
LLMs now write heuristics as code inside an evolutionary loop.
FunSearch \cite{romera2024funsearch}, EoH \cite{liu2024evolution} and ReEvo \cite{ye2024reevo} established this approach.
Later methods measure and maintain the diversity of the code population \cite{dat2025hsevo},
organize the candidates in a search tree \cite{zheng2025monte},
optimize several objectives such as quality and efficiency \cite{yao2025multi},
evolve a set of complementary heuristics instead of one \cite{liu2026eohs},
and generate complete metaheuristics \cite{vanstein2025llamea}.
HeuriGym provides a benchmark for LLM-written heuristics \cite{chen2026heurigym}, and two surveys cover the field \cite{liu2026systematic,zhang2026systematic}.
In scheduling, LLMs rewrite the operators of an EA for flexible job shops during the run \cite{liao2025online},
build portfolios of dispatching rules with a learned selector for dynamic shops \cite{zhou2026dsevolve},
generate dispatching rules in an actor--critic setup \cite{may2026llm},
produce dispatching rules for human--robot collaborative manufacturing \cite{huang2025leveraging},
and supply heuristics to NSGA-II \cite{deb2002nsga2} for multi-objective flexible job shops \cite{fornies2025remoh}.

\textbf{Neural construction.}
Deep reinforcement learning trains policies that build schedules directly, for example with graph neural networks for flexible job shops \cite{liu2025flexible}.

In most of these works, a learned rule or program is judged by the quality of the schedules it produces.
In \SDD, the rule is part of a decoder: the search starts from it and departs from it through a few genes that mark deviations (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
We therefore judge a rule by the rosters that random deviations from it produce (Section~\ref{sec:method:proxy}),
and Section~\ref{sec:exp-static} compares GP, neural networks and LLMs as learners for this score.
The closest work in spirit is \cite{liao2025online}, which also uses an LLM to change how a GA explores;
it rewrites the variation operators, whereas we change the rule inside the decoder.

\subsection{Rescheduling and Multi-Objective Rostering}
\label{sec:related:resched}

When a schedule is disrupted, rescheduling methods revise the rest of it.
Shen and Yao \cite{shen2015dfjsp} solve dynamic flexible job shops with proactive--reactive MOEAs that include a stability objective,
and they seed the initial population with the previous schedule, a repaired schedule and their mutants.
Recent work applies deep reinforcement learning to event-driven predictive--reactive multi-objective rescheduling \cite{duan2025deep},
and combines a competitive EA with double Q-learning for robust scheduling under disruptions \cite{zhao2024dql}.
In personnel rostering, rerostering after staff absences has been solved by GAs on permutations that are decoded into rosters \cite{moz2007genetic}
and by other evolutionary methods \cite{maenhout2011evolutionary}.
A recent review notes that most rostering studies combine their criteria into a weighted sum,
and surveys the smaller set of methods that keep the trade-off between criteria \cite{bodvarsdottir2026review}.

The evolutionary methods above use the old schedule as a reference in the objective or as a starting point of the search.
\SDD instead puts the old schedule into the decoder (Section~\ref{sec:exp-rerostering}):
the old schedule becomes the rule, and every genotype departs from it in only a few shifts.
We keep two objectives, the number of changes and the imbalance, under a resource budget, and report the hypervolume.

\subsection{When Does \RDD Help?}

\RDD helps when two conditions hold: the rule is good, and good solutions lie within reach of it.
The first condition is clear in the rostering results.
With the default rule, \RDD is not better than \dec{5}; with learned rules at the same deviation rate, it is (Table~\ref{tab:static-main}).
On FJSP, a standard dispatching rule is good enough, but the bias toward its first choice is needed: the per-step encoding PS, which uses the same rule order without that bias, is worse than random keys (Table~\ref{tab:fjsp-main}).

The second condition explains where \RDD gains least.
In the E group of the rostering instances, all robots are of one type.
For the good rosters that \dec{5} finds on the five E training instances, we counted the shifts at which each roster differs from the rule's first choice.
At $\drate = 0.1$, the median distance to the nearest good roster is about equal to the number of slots,
and on the test instances the learned rules are then significantly worse than \dec{5} in this group.
From $\drate = 0.2$ on, they are no longer worse, but they are not significantly better either.
On FJSP, the gain is smallest on the edata instances, which are close to the classical job shop, and 8 of the 12 instances on which RK wins come from this set.

\subsection{The Deviation Rate and the Theory}

Several predictions of Section~\ref{sec:theory} hold on both problems.
The deviation rate $\drate = \qon\dens$, not the number of slots, determines performance.
The search keeps the share of active slots close to $\qon$, as the binomial prior of the model assumes.
With too few slots, good solutions are out of reach, as in the E group at $\drate = 0.1$.
On FJSP, the best rate grows with the budget.

One result does not fit the model.
On rostering, a high rate ($\drate = 0.4$) is best already at 465 evaluations, although the model predicts that a small budget calls for no more deviations than the distance to the nearest good solution.
Part of this gap comes from the second objective.
The rosters on a front lie much farther from the rule than the nearest good roster:
on four training instances, the nearest good roster differs from the rule in fewer than five shifts, but the median roster on the first front of \dec{5} differs in 21 to 35.
As Section~\ref{sec:theory:multi} shows, the best number of slots then follows the mean distance to the front.
In the model, the rest of the gap can only come from cheap unnecessary deviations.
The rostering decoder is highly neutral (Section~\ref{sec:motivation}), so a deviation that is not needed may often change little, but we have not measured this cost directly.
On FJSP, unnecessary deviations are clearly costly: HV drops significantly when the rate is too high (Table~\ref{tab:fjsp-rate}).

The model also predicts the direction of the budget effect but not its size.
It predicts that the best number of slots grows by a factor of about $1/\qon$ from small to large budgets, \ie by 1.7 to 5 for our three values of $\qon$;
on FJSP, the best rate grows by a factor of 1.2 to 1.7, less than predicted for every $\qon$.

In practice, the deviation rate must therefore be set for each problem.
The best rate is about 0.4 for rostering and 0.1 to 0.2 for FJSP at moderate and large budgets.
A few settings evaluated on training instances are enough, as Section~\ref{sec:method:k} shows.

\subsection{Choosing a Rule Learner}

Under the proxy score, GP, a neural network trained by ES, and an LLM reach similar test performance, while imitation of good rosters is clearly worse.
The learners differ in cost and reliability.
The LLM needs the fewest proxy evaluations, but its rules vary most between runs,
and five of the nine LLM rules fail on one test instance because they ignore the gene type and thus remove freedom that the encoding was meant to provide.
The ES-trained network is the most stable across runs.
When an LLM writes the rule, its rules should therefore be checked on instances with tight resources, and the interface should keep the inputs that carry the genotype's choices.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One problem family for rule learning.} The learned rules and the main tests use one rostering problem, with instances from one generator and 15 training instances (five per group).
  With five instances per group, the deviation rate could only be selected for all groups together.
  \item \textbf{Baselines.} The rostering baselines are the decoders \dec{0}--\dec{5} of the scheduling software that motivated this work.
  We did not compare with other metaheuristics that also search near a construction heuristic, such as large neighborhood search or iterated greedy.
  \item \textbf{Robustness checks.} The final rate $\drate = 0.4$ was tested on the test instances only with NSGA-II and 465 evaluations; the checks with MOEA/D and 2000 evaluations used $\drate = 0.2$.
  \item \textbf{Limited reach.} \RDD searches near the rule: every decision without a deviation follows the rule. In the model of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, $\K$ slots cannot reach a solution that differs from the rule in more than $\K$ decisions (Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}). \RDD therefore cannot make up for a rule that is far from all good solutions.
  \item \textbf{FJSP.} We used a fixed rule and a generic MOEA. The makespans remain about 10\% above the best-known values, and \RDD has not been combined with the local search that specialized FJSP algorithms use.
\end{itemize}
